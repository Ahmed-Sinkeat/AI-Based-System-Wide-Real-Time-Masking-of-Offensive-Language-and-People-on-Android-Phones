1Summary

We build an Android app that covers offensive words and images of people on the screen, in every app, with all AI running on the phone, and that covers new content before the user sees it. Research so far shows this is possible on a normal, not rooted, Android 14+ phone, and that no existing product or published system does all of it.

Contribution: a system-wide, on-device, Arabic-first filter that covers offensive words (in text and in images) and people, and hides new content until it is checked.

4.1 Offensive language detection

Automatic detection of abusive and hateful language is a mature research area. Schmidt and Wiegand survey the early methods, from word lists and n-gram features to learned classifiers [72]. Davidson et al. show that separating hate speech from merely offensive language is hard, and that word lists alone flag many harmless posts [71]. The OffensEval shared tasks fixed common labels for offensive language and attracted hundreds of teams; the 2020 edition added Arabic, Danish, Greek and Turkish, with pre-trained transformers on top [73], [74]. Most systems decide for a whole post; the toxic-spans task asks instead which words are offensive [60], which is what a screen filter needs to cover only those words.

Gap. These systems label posts offline, usually on a server, for moderators. None of them works on whatever text is on the user's screen, in real time, on the phone, and marks the exact words to cover.

4.2 Arabic offensive language

Arabic adds dialects, spelling variants, letter normalization, elongation and Arabizi (Arabic in Latin letters). Mubarak et al. built one of the first Arabic obscene-word lists from social media [1]. The OSACT4 shared task (2020) created the standard Arabic offensive-tweet benchmark [2]; strong systems relied on careful preprocessing [3], [4], and the winning ALT system used character n-grams with linear models [64]. OSACT5 (2022) extended it to fine-grained hate speech [75]. Bensalem et al. review 54 Arabic toxic-language datasets and note that most come from Twitter and a few dialects [76]; Arabizi needs its own handling [5]. Arabic pre-trained encoders such as MARBERT [61] and CAMeLBERT [62] are strong on these tasks. Recent work targets safety for Arabic LLMs and memes: ArabicDialectSafety [56], the ArGuard shared task [57] and FanarGuard [59]. A distilled Arabic toxicity model made for phones exists (HAYAA-student, message level only) [15]. Open Arabic word lists exist but are small and scattered [6], [20]–[24].

What we reuse: the OSACT data for training and testing; Lucene's Arabic normalization rules [17]; all open word lists, combined (§5.4); multi-phrase matching in one pass with Aho–Corasick [18], [94].

Gap. No public Arabic resource is both word-level (which words to cover) and small enough for a low-cost phone. Context-dependent words such as كلب (dog) or كافر (unbeliever) are offensive only in some sentences, which a list cannot decide.

4.3 Small text models for phones

Knowledge distillation trains a small "student" to copy a large "teacher" [77]; DistilBERT keeps most of BERT's quality at 40% less size [78], and the HAYAA student keeps 96.7% agreement with a teacher 68× larger [15]. Large language models now label text well: ChatGPT beat crowd workers on several annotation tasks [79], and open guard models such as Qwen3Guard cover 119 languages [54]. Decision models such as Jev return a fixed, typed choice with a confidence instead of free text, which is fast and easy to threshold [49]; open alternatives include Laya, Von, Kev and GLiNER2.5-Decide [50]–[53]. Jev runs only as a cloud API, so we take the idea, not the model: our phone model gives a typed answer (category + confidence + which words), and large decision and guard models are used only offline, as teachers that label training data. Multilingual models spend most of their size on the word table; vocabulary trimming removes unused tokens with no retraining [63]. Small candidates include multilingual encoders (mmBERT [55], multilingual E5 [69], EmbeddingGemma [67]), static embeddings (Model2Vec [65], [66]) and small generative models (Gemma 3 270M [68]). Quantization to 8-bit integers shrinks and speeds models on phones [86], but must be checked: our own int8 people model found nobody (§5.3).

Gap. Teacher → student has not been applied to word-level Arabic offensive language under a phone budget (≤ 100 MB RAM, ≤ 50 ms per message). That is our RQ5.

4.4 Text inside images

Memes, thumbnails and video subtitles carry text that screen readers never see. PP-OCR introduced an ultra-light OCR pipeline for mobile [80]; PaddleOCR 3.0 and PP-OCRv5 add multilingual recognition with models under 100 M parameters [81], [16], and an open-source Android app already runs it [41]. Google's on-device ML Kit OCR does not support Arabic [33]. In our design, text read from an image goes through the same word list and text model as screen text (§4.3). The ArGuard task studies harmful Arabic memes, but with server-side models [57].

Gap. Reading Arabic text in images on a low-cost phone, fast enough to cover words while scrolling is not measured anywhere we found. That is our RQ6 (step P0d).

4.5 Finding and segmenting people

Real-time detection began with one-stage detectors such as YOLO [82]. Mask R-CNN added a pixel mask for each object [83], and YOLACT made instance masks real-time [84]. Today's small real-time segmenters are the YOLO11/YOLO26 family [10] and RF-DETR [8], [11]. SAM 3 segments objects from a text prompt such as "person" and can label training data [9]. COCO is the standard benchmark, with small, medium and large object sizes [7]. Small objects are a known weakness; slicing the image into tiles (SAHI) raises small-object accuracy by 5–7 AP points without retraining [85], which is our v4 method. AI Benchmark shows how widely phone chips differ, and that phone AI units often do not help in practice [87], which matches our test phone (§5.3).

Gap. Benchmarks use photos and fast GPUs. There are no published results for people on phone screens (feeds, grids, thumbnails) on a low-cost phone, which is why we built our own tests (M4).

4.6 Hiding sensitive images

Most image filters classify the whole image as safe or not, for example pornography classifiers [88] and the browser model nsfwjs used by a competitor (§4.8). Two findings shape how we hide:

  • Light blur is not enough. McPherson et al. recognised faces and objects through blur and pixelation with deep learning [89]. We use solid covers.
  • Even a glimpse counts. People understood the meaning of pictures shown for only 13 ms [90]. A filter that shows content and then blurs it has already failed. We cover first, then check.
Gap. Image-level filters blur or block a whole picture after it is shown. We cover only the person's shape, and before it is shown.

4.7 The Android platform

A normal app cannot change how Android draws the screen. Three platform features make our design possible:

FeatureWhat it givesSource
Accessibility serviceOn-screen text from every app, and an event when it changes[25], [35]
Per-character boxes (Android 8+)The exact screen position of each word, so only the word is covered; Chrome provides them for web pages[26], [34]
Window picture (Android 14+)A picture of one app's window without our covers, at most one every 333 ms[27], [28]
Accessibility overlayTrusted covers that may be fully solid while touches pass through[27], [29]

The limits exist for security. Cloak and Dagger showed that an app with the overlay and accessibility permissions could take full control of the phone's interface [91]. Android then capped see-through overlays of normal apps at 80% opacity [29], [30], requires consent for each screen-capture session [31], and Google Play restricts non-accessibility uses of the accessibility API [32].

Consequence. The accessibility path is the only non-root way to get solid, touch-through covers and a picture that excludes them. It needs Android 14+, and it is the reason our app asks for one permission only and has no internet permission.

8Ethics, privacy and AI use

  • Privacy by design: screen content is processed in memory on the phone; the app has no internet permission and saves nothing.
  • Public data: reviews and issues are reported in aggregate, without user names. A competitor's AGPL code was read for ideas, not copied.
  • Licenses of every word list, dataset and model are recorded; one list's author will be asked for permission.
  • Private training screenshots (v5) are never published or sent to outside services.
  • AI tools helped with searching, scripts and drafting; all measurements come from our own runs, and the team checks and owns the content. The department allows AI tools.
